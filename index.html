A data story about fitness apps and privacy

This is Millie.
She went for a run.

How much can a stranger learn about someone from publicly accessible information, starting from a single run, and what can that person do to limit it?

Millie isn’t real. She, her friends and her profiles are invented; only the streets are real. Every claim below is labeled , or . How we label claims.

The run

Thursday, 7:04 a.m. Millie leaves the Barnard campus block on Broadway and heads west on 116th Street toward the Hudson. Her watch logs her position every second or two. When she saves the run, her app’s default settings make it visible to everyone.

Map: Morningside Heights in Manhattan. A dot moves west from Broadway along 116th Street into Riverside Park.

She drops into Riverside Park and turns north. Nothing about this is unusual, and that’s the point. Nothing on this screen is secret. It’s exactly what she chose to post.

Map: the dot travels north through Riverside Park, then east along West 122nd Street.

Across 122nd Street, then south on Amsterdam Avenue. Every point carries a timestamp, so the activity shows not only where she went but when she was there, down to the minute.

Map: the dot heads south on Amsterdam Avenue and into Morningside Park.

Down the stairs into Morningside Park, the steepest part of the loop, and back to Broadway: km in . One ordinary run. Now let’s see what a stranger could add up from it.

Map: the completed loop, from Barnard through Riverside Park, Amsterdam Avenue and Morningside Park, back to Broadway.

2

Where she starts and ends

Every run has a start and an end. On Millie’s very first upload, both are visible. Strava starts hiding the first and last 200 m only for activities saved after the first one.

Map, zoomed in on Broadway near West 117th Street: one start marker and one end marker, a few metres apart.

One point means little. Here are the starts and ends of all runs from the past year. Phone GPS wobbles, but the points pile up on the same stretch of sidewalk. Repetition turns a guess into a pattern.

Map: a tight cluster of start and end points on Broadway at the Barnard campus block.

Strava’s default hides the first and last 200 m of each run, so the endpoints vanish. But the visible tracks now begin on a ring around the hidden spot, and the ring has a center.

Map: endpoints removed. Small markers show where each visible track begins, scattered around a 200-metre circle centred on the same block.

A stronger option hides the map near an address you choose. Unlike the other two map options, it also applies to past runs. The hidden area grows, but it’s still drawn around the place it protects.

Map: a larger hidden circle around the campus block. Visible tracks begin at its edge.

Even with every endpoint hidden, the shape of the run leaks. Her loop climbs from m by the river to m on the ridge. Researchers predicted a user’s city or borough from elevation profiles alone, with 59.59% to 99.80% success depending on what else was known (Meteriz-Yildiran et al., 2024).

What the data can’t say is which building she lives in. A start point is where someone pressed “record,” not necessarily their front door. Our story puts Millie’s starts on a campus block, never at a residence.

3

When she runs

Timestamps tell their own story. This grid counts Millie’s runs by weekday and hour. Two patterns stand out: Tuesday and Thursday around 7 a.m., and Sunday mid-afternoon.

Laid out across the year, the rhythm holds: of runs land in the same three weekly slots. A schedule this steady also marks the hours she’s predictably out.

Chart: a year-long calendar strip with one mark per run, regularly spaced three times a week.

Then there are the gaps: late December, early May, mid-July. It’s tempting to explain them. Travel? Exams? Illness? The data can’t tell you which.

Places repeat, too. Every run passes through Riverside Park or Morningside Park, on the same few routes.
