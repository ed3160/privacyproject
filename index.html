A data story about fitness apps and privacy

She went for a run.

By Iqra, Rachel & Esha · Privacy in a Networked World · Columbia University / Barnard College

Millie posted one run. We wanted to see how much a stranger could figure out about her from it, and what she could do to limit that.

2

Where she starts and ends

On Millie’s first upload, you can see exactly where her run started and ended. Strava only starts hiding the first and last 200 m on activities saved after the first one.

Map, zoomed in on Broadway near West 117th Street: one start marker and one end marker, a few metres apart.

One run on its own doesn’t say much. This map shows where all of her runs from the past year started and ended. GPS isn’t perfect, but almost all of them land on the same stretch of Broadway.

Map: a tight cluster of start and end points on Broadway at the Barnard campus block.

With Strava’s default setting, the first and last 200 m of each run are hidden. But the visible part of each run now starts around the edge of a circle, and the middle of that circle is still where she started.

Map: endpoints removed. Small markers show where each visible track begins, scattered around a 200-metre circle centred on the same block.

Strava also lets you hide the map around an address you choose. This is the only map setting that also applies to past runs. It hides a bigger area, but that area is still centered on the place it’s protecting.

Map: a larger hidden circle around the campus block. Visible tracks begin at its edge.

Even with the start and end hidden, the run’s elevation chart is still public. Her loop goes from m by the river up to m at the top of the hill.

That might not seem like much, but researchers were able to guess a user’s city or borough from elevation data alone, with 59.59% to 99.80% accuracy depending on what else they knew (Meteriz-Yildiran et al., 2024).

What the data can’t tell you is which building she lives in. A start point is just where someone pressed “record,” which isn’t always their front door. In our story, Millie’s runs start on a campus block, not at a home.

3

When she runs

Timestamps show when someone runs, not just where. This grid counts Millie’s runs by day and hour. She usually runs Tuesday and Thursday around 7 a.m., and Sunday in the afternoon.

Over the whole year, of her runs happen in those same three time slots. A schedule this regular also shows when she’s likely to be out of the house.

Chart: a year-long calendar strip with one mark per run, regularly spaced three times a week.

There are also a few gaps where she didn’t run: late December, early May and mid-July. It could be travel, exams or being sick, but the data doesn’t tell you which.

She also goes to the same places. Every run goes through Riverside Park or Morningside Park, and she sticks to a few routes.

Look at what repeating does. One run is a dot on a map. Fifty runs are a pattern, and a year of them is close to a profile. Each new run makes the earlier guesses firmer.

  • Home area: a single start point could be anywhere. starts on one block are a neighbourhood.
  • Routine: one Thursday run is a coincidence. runs in the same three time slots are a schedule.
  • Gaps: a missing week means nothing until you know she almost never misses one. Then the silence says something too.

Each run is a dot. A year of runs is a profile. Next: what Millie would expect from sharing these runs, and what actually happens.

4

What she’d expect, and what happens

Privacy researchers call this contextual integrity: information comes with unwritten rules about where it’s meant to go (Nissenbaum, 2004). We can’t know exactly what every Strava user expects, so the left column is our best guess at a reasonable norm. The right column is what the data lets a stranger do.

Where she starts and ends

What a runner would expect

“I’m sharing where I ran with other runners. It shows my route, not where I live.”

How that breaks

Run after run starts and ends on the same block. Even with the first and last 200 m hidden, the gap in the track points back to that block.

When she runs

What a runner would expect

“My workout times are for friends comparing training, not for anyone tracking when I’m out.”

How that breaks

Timestamps repeat on a weekly rhythm, so a stranger can tell when she’s probably outside, and a gap can show when she’s away.

Who she is

What a runner would expect

“A fitness profile stays in a fitness context. Nobody should be able to connect it to my school or my career.”

How that breaks

A public bio that names her school and year, plus a neighbourhood she runs in every week, is enough to link her fitness profile to other pages about her.

5

Small facts that add up

None of these facts is sensitive by itself. Together they narrow a crowd down to a few people (Sweeney, 2000) (Ohm, 2010).

Her area, school and class year point to one person.

Each fact is public on its own page. Together they match fewer people than any one does, though we have not measured how few.

No names needed

Say a stranger has no idea who Millie is. They still can read when she is usually out of the house, Tuesday and Thursday mornings around seven, where she will be on a Sunday afternoon, that for about three weeks around New Year’s she wasn’t running, and that her heart rate hit 181 bpm on the stairs. That is a schedule, a location and a hint about her body, and nobody has been identified.

Sensitive information can be revealed without anyone being identified. You don’t have to know who someone is to know when they’re not home.

6

What public cameras could add

Strava data says where a runner was and when. A public camera records who was at a place and when. Put together, they could connect a name and a face to a route.

  1. Cameras exist

    Webcams, city and transit cameras and tourist cams already watch public places.

  2. A run passes by

    Her activity shows a place and a time, such as the block by Barnard at 7:04 a.m.

  3. Time + place + camera

    A camera covering that spot could show a runner there at that minute.

Her run’s date, start time and route are public.

On a default-settings activity, anyone can see them.

A camera covering her usual block could have caught her.

Her runs cluster on one block (Part 2) and follow a weekly schedule (Part 3), so we can guess where she’ll be and when.

A camera frame proves it was her.

Many people pass the same place. A runner on camera at the right minute is a lead, not proof.

Why there is no demo. We chose not to show a camera match. Doing it with real footage could help someone target a real person, so we describe it here and leave it out of the site. Our report explains this choice.
