Background and Motivation
When our group was deciding on a project, we wanted to start with something familiar: the apps we use every day. Rather than looking at privacy as an abstract issue, we searched for the answer to the question: how much could we actually learn about the privacy risks hiding inside the apps we already use? As we analyzed our favorite apps, we quickly realized how many seemingly ordinary products raised questions about privacy. The list of possible examples kept growing, and so did the discussion around what information these platforms make available, what users choose to share, and what someone else might be able to figure out from it.
Strava stood out because it is hard to imagine what could be sensitive about a fitness app. But the more we considered the information in a single Strava activity, or in activities over time, the more surprising the picture became. Where and when someone runs, how often they run in certain places, and what they share on their profile can reveal a “pattern of life.” While talking this through, we remembered a scene from the Netflix series “You” in which serial killer protagonist Joe learns his target’s routine and patterns and follows her on her runs. The series is fiction, but it depicts a concrete risk: a public activity feed can hand a stranger the same information. Combined with other information available online, these details can show far more about a person than the runner ever meant to share.
This led us to look more closely at the ways information can become more revealing when it is connected with other information. In a networked world, data does not have to be sensitive on its own to become sensitive with aggregate data. Once information is linked, it can reveal where someone goes, who they spend time with, and what their routine looks like.
We were particularly interested in the role of opt-in privacy features. Many platforms provide users with settings that can limit what others can see, but these protections only help if users know they exist, understand what they do, and choose to enable them. This creates an important gap between having privacy controls and actually being able to make informed privacy decisions.
Ultimately, our motivation is not to discourage people from using Strava or other networked services. Instead, we want people to better understand the information they make available and feel more confident making choices about their privacy. By showing how publicly accessible information can be pieced together to reveal details about an individual, we hope to make privacy risks easier to recognize and encourage users to think more carefully about what they choose to make public.

Problem Space and Related Research
Our project addresses three problems with how users share data on apps like Strava. First, most people do not realize how much their data can reveal. Second, they do not know what someone could actually do with it. Third, they do not know how to protect themselves, especially using existing opt-in protections.
These problems are not unique to Strava users. According to a 2023 Pew Research Center survey, 67% of Americans say they have little or no understanding of how companies use the data collected about them. Many also struggle to make informed privacy decisions: 56% say they frequently click “agree” to privacy policies without reading them, and 61% say these policies are ineffective at explaining how companies use their data.
Strava’s 2017 global heatmap shows all three problems. It was built from more than 1 billion activities and 3 trillion latitude and longitude points. In early 2018, open-source intelligence analysts found that it exposed the layouts, patrol routes, and perimeters of secret U.S. and allied military bases, based on the running patterns of people using the app. The soldiers did not necessarily know how much their runs gave away, that strangers could turn that data into a map of a base, or that privacy settings could have limited what was exposed.
Research shows the same risk for individuals. One study found that researchers could predict a user’s city or borough from an elevation profile alone, with success rates ranging from 59.59% to 99.80% depending on the information available. Another study found that machine-learning models could re-identify approximately 95% of adults from physical activity data even after protected health information had been removed. Data does not have to identify someone directly to reveal sensitive information about them.
Contextual integrity gives us our main argument, as Nissenbaum’s (2004) work says that every piece of information has rules that fit the place where a person shares it. In our context, a runner who posts a route for friends expects the route to stay in a fitness setting. A stranger, on the other hand, who uses that information and juxtaposes that route to a LinkedIn page breaks Nissenbaum’s rules, even if each piece is public.
Re-identification research shows the same problem, as seen in Ohm (2010), that removing names does not make data anonymous, because other facts fill the gap. Sweeney (2000) found that ZIP code, birth date, and sex identify 87% of people in the United States. We use this idea throughout our interactive storytelling piece, where we use fictional data, with an awareness that every small fact can join to another until the person is clear.
Current policy puts much of the safety work on the user, as many apps use notice (showing you the terms and conditions) and choice (allowing you to either accept them or discontinue proceeding). Solove (2013) calls this the privacy self-management problem. We believe that a click does not show that a person understood the risk. Opt-in controls can make the gap larger because the user must find the setting and turn it on.
Our website addresses this gap by showing the potential harm first, followed by the setting that limits it. We want users to make an informed choice without asking them to stop using Strava entirely. Our findings also aim to support a broader argument for privacy-protective defaults that put more responsibility on companies rather than individual users.

2.1 Strava’s Existing Privacy Controls
It is important to note that Strava already offers multiple privacy controls that allow users to limit what information they share. For example, users can set their activities to “Everyone,” “Followers,” or “Only You.” They can also control who sees their profile and choose to hide parts of their running routes, including the start and end of an activity or the entire map. Map Visibility is a particularly relevant feature, as it allows users to hide portions of their routes near a specific address or hide the beginning and end of all future activities. However, we immediately noticed that this setting is not widely-advertised and requires clicking through multiple sections to locate it in the iPhone app. 
It is also worth mentioning that Strava automatically hides the first and last 200 meters of future activity maps by default when a user logs into the app. While this provides some protection, it does not completely eliminate the risk of revealing sensitive locations. For example, if someone repeatedly starts and ends their runs in the same neighborhood, it probably isn’t too difficult to narrow down where they live.
Interestingly enough, while Strava automatically hides portions of users’ routes, it also sets both their profile and activity visibility to “Everyone” by default. According to Strava's own documentation, this means that someone’s profile is publicly accessible even to people without a Strava account and “may be indexed by search engines and consequently appear in search results” (Strava, 2026). In other words, users who have never changed their privacy settings may be sharing information with a much larger audience than they realize.
This brings us back to the privacy self-management problem discussed by Solove (2013). Although Strava provides users with tools to protect their data, these protections are only effective if users understand what data they're sharing and how the different settings work. Even when certain protections are enabled by default, users may not understand their limitations. We believe that privacy should not depend entirely on users knowing which settings to look for or anticipating all the ways their data could be used.

Research Question and Methodology
The central question of our project is: How much can someone learn about a person from publicly accessible information, starting from a single Strava run, and what can that person do to limit it?
To explore this question, we are developing an interactive website that follows a fictional runner along a Strava route. As readers progress through the experience, they encounter different pieces of information that could be inferred from the runner’s activity and publicly available information elsewhere online.
In order to prevent harm, we use a fictional persona so that we do not expose any real person. Our original plan was to record a sample run ourselves and use only information that anyone can find without a login. This approach follows the Association of Internet Researchers’ guidance, which asks researchers to weigh the risk to people against the benefit of the work (Franzke et al., 2020). It also aligns with Boyd and Crawford (2012), who warn that data is not free to use simply because it is public.
We intend to demonstrate privacy risks without publishing real residential addresses, identifying unsuspecting individuals, or providing detailed instructions for targeting another person.

3.1 Fictional Persona and Sample Run
Our fictional runner is Millie. She is a Barnard student who studies cognitive science. She grew up in New York and has run at least twice a week for the past year. Her Strava profile has 10 followers, which include friends and family, and two profiles she doesn’t recognize. Her profile uses Strava’s default settings. Having an account that utilizes the default settings shows what a typical new account exposes.

3.2 Data Collection and Analysis
We examined public Strava profile pages, the Strava Global Heatmap, LinkedIn, and Barnard and Columbia web pages. We also drew on survey research on how Americans view the data that social media and other companies collect about them (Faverio, 2023; McClain et al., 2023), and on studies of re-identification from fitness and health app data (Meteriz-Yildiran et al., 2024; Na et al., 2018). To access sites like LinkedIn, we logged into free accounts for these platforms, because any observer can do the same at no cost. The street-level heatmap also requires a login to a free Strava account. 
We simulated the route, the persona, the year of activity history, and the cross-platform profiles, as well as collected documentation of Strava’s settings and published research. We excluded every real person’s route, name, photo, and address, and we made sure that we did not retain data about any real individual we saw while studying the platforms.
Despite what we mentioned in our previous assignment, we did not use the Strava API, as the API is now only available to registered developers. Previously, it was open to hobbyists, but now the new application starts with access to a single athlete (TryTerra, 2026). Strava’s API changes of November 2024 also limit third-party apps to showing a user’s activity data only to that user (Pal, 2024). The API is essentially useless to a stranger. 
In order to organize our work, we label every claim on the site with one of three levels. Something observed means the information appears directly on a public page, like reading someone’s bio. Inferred means we derived it from observed facts, and we state the evidence that supports it, such as someone under 25 who frequently runs around Morningside Heights is very likely to be a Columbia student. Last, unsupported means the evidence does not establish the claim. A starting point near a residential building might suggest a home location, but does not, for certain, establish that the person in question lives there. Repetition is solidifying, as one run’s information isn’t very robust on its own, but many runs at the same time and place support a pattern.

3.3 Ethical Considerations
Our demonstration is designed to educate users about privacy risks rather than expose information about real individuals. We explain how certain types of information can be combined while limiting details that could facilitate stalking or unwanted identification.

Our ethical rules are as follows:
1. Only fictional people appear on the site and in this report.
2. We will not publish any real residential addresses. The route uses real public streets, and we use Barnard College as the generalized start and end block.
3. The persona’s profile image is an illustration or placeholder and not a real person.
4. Cross-platform examples use fictional profiles that we will build, which means we do not link to or screenshot real LinkedIn pages.
5. Our informational site will explain what each kind of data reveals and which setting limits it. However, it does not give step-by-step instructions or name tools for targeting a person.
6. We excluded the camera-matching demonstration. See the Camera Matching section below.

3.4 Preliminary Findings: What a Strava Run Can Reveal
Our proposed investigation focuses on six categories of information: home location, frequently visited places, daily schedule, personal identity, potential camera matching, and fitness information. These categories illustrate how information that initially appears harmless can become more revealing when combined with other data.
3.4.1 Home Location
The start and end points of a Strava activity can reveal where someone lives, particularly when multiple activities begin and end in the same area.
Millie’s year of runs gives a clear example. Most activities start and end in the same place near campus. Meteriz-Yildiran et al. (2024) found that researchers could predict a user’s city or borough from an elevation profile with success rates of 59.59% to 99.80%. Strava’s Map Visibility controls can hide the start and end points that point to her home, but they do not remove every location signal, because elevation data can still place a runner. The address option applies to past activities, and the other two apply only to future ones, so Millie’s earlier runs are still exposed until she uses the address option (Strava, n.d.-a).

3.4.2 Daily Routine and Frequently Visited Locations
Repeated routes and activity timestamps can reveal patterns in someone’s schedule, including locations they visit regularly and times when they may be away from home.
Millie runs Tuesday and Thursday mornings and Sunday afternoons, which becomes a very obvious pattern to anyone looking at her timestamps. The same data shows when she is likely away from home, and an unexplained gap suggests travelling or some other disturbance in daily life (death of a loved one, academic stressors, etc.). Setting activities to Followers or Only You, or hiding maps, limits outside observers from seeing when you are free, when you are most likely to be outside running, and where you will be, before you’re even there (Strava, n.d.-b).

3.4.3 Personal Identity and Cross-Platform Information
A public name or profile detail on Strava can potentially be connected to information on LinkedIn, school websites, organization pages, or other online profiles. Each additional source can reveal information that was not apparent from the original fitness activity.
Millie’s profile shows her name, Barnard, her graduation year (2027), and Brooklyn, which shows that beyond currently attending college in NYC, she grew up in NYC as well. We build a fictional LinkedIn-style page that lists her major, class year, and an internship. Each source looks harmless alone, but joined together, not only is her identity very clear, but she also becomes more predictable to track down. Despite each piece being public, put all together, contextual integrity explains how information carries the norms of the context where it was shared (Nissenbaum, 2004), so when Millie shares runs with friends in a fitness context and her school and major with recruiters in a professional one, a stranger who can view both can utilize her data with malicious intent (Nissenbaum, 2004). The same logic as Sweeney (2000) applies, as Sweeney’s work found that ZIP code, birth date, and sex identify 87% of people in the United States. In our example, the area of her runs, her school, and her class year play the same role.

3.4.4 Camera Matching and Physical Identification
Our original proposal also considered whether publicly accessible camera imagery and facial recognition could contribute to identifying someone during their run.
We removed this part of our project, as it needs footage of real people, and facial matching of bystanders cannot be done safely without their consent. It would be difficult to have, or to fake, our fictional Millie be recorded on street cameras. We will keep the lessons and the information to be learned about public camera footage in the aggregate exposure finding, because the two sources fill each other’s gaps. Strava shows when and where Millie runs. A public camera on that route can show what she looks like, what she wears, and whether she runs alone. A stalker could use the pair to find her at a predictable time and place,  an attacker could choose the most isolated stretch of her route and the minute she passes it, a burglar could read the same schedule as the hours her home is empty, a hacker could link her face to her name and profiles, which makes phishing messages or impersonation more convincing, etc. The message is that these sources combined connect a public fitness log to a face and a physical location, opening doors to harm.

3.4.5 Fitness and Health-Related Inferences
Strava activities can also reveal information about someone’s physical activity through metrics such as pace, distance, frequency, and other statistics they choose to share. Over time, these measurements may allow observers to draw broader conclusions about their habits or physical condition.
We include pace, distance, frequency, and heart rate if shared. An observer can estimate Millie’s fitness level and her consistency, and a gap in activity can signal illness, exams, or travel, so we label health conclusions as uncertain. Na et al. (2018) re-identified about 95% of adults in a national physical activity dataset by combining activity data with demographic data. Setting activities to Followers limits this visibility (Strava, n.d.-b).
Health and fitness apps, including Strava, put this data at risk outside the public profile. In the United States, HIPAA covers doctors and insurers, but it does not cover many fitness trackers and health apps (Federal Trade Commission, 2022). Many of these apps pass user data to other companies. A BMJ study of 24 popular health apps found that 19 of them (79%) sent user data to outside companies, and those companies advertised that they could pass it on to advertisers and a consumer credit reporting agency (Grundy et al., 2019). Heart rate data can also expose people in person. In 2018, an investigator used public sessions on the Polar fitness app, which list heart rate next to route, date, and time, to find nearly 6,500 users at more than 200 sensitive sites and trace many of them to their likely homes (Postma, 2018). 
For the purposes of our site, Millie's heart rate being shared tells a stranger how fit she is and when she runs hardest. Another example is that if an attacker can see where she might get winded/out of breath when her heart rate increases, they can use that period of exhaustion as a window for a physical attack. A breach or a sale of that data can tie it to her name, and an attacker can use health details to make phishing messages more convincing.

3.5 The Combined Privacy Risk
The larger issue is not necessarily any single piece of information, but what happens when different pieces are connected. A running route might reveal an approximate location, repeated activities might reveal a routine, and a public profile might link that routine to a particular identity.
Strava, by default, includes eligible activities in the Global Heatmap and Metro unless the user opts out (Strava, n.d.-b). This is the mechanism behind the 2018 military base exposure (Bogle, 2018; Hern, 2018). Service members who ran with default settings fed their runs into the public heatmap. In remote places such as Syria and Afghanistan, where few local people use Strava, their activity stood out as bright lines that traced the outlines and patrol routes of the bases. Millie's route runs through populated streets like Amsterdam, so her runs blend into many others. Researchers have noted that living in a densely populated area makes person-specific tracking much harder (Childs et al., 2023). 
Together, these observations illustrate why information that appears harmless in isolation may create meaningful privacy risks when aggregated.

Interactive Website Design and Progress

4.1 Website Structure
Our website will follow Millie as she completes a run, with each section revealing another piece of information that someone could potentially learn about her from Strava and other publicly available sources. We want the experience to feel like the reader is gradually putting together a picture of who Millie is, rather than simply reading a list of privacy risks.
We plan to begin by introducing Millie’s fictional Strava profile and showing her running route. At first, the information will seem relatively harmless, including her name, running distance, and the path she takes. As the reader scrolls, a dot will move along the route on the side of the screen, while the accompanying text introduces new information that could be inferred from her activity.
The first section will focus on location, explaining how the starting and ending points of a run could suggest where someone lives. We will then introduce Millie’s fictional history of runs to demonstrate how repeated routes and timestamps can reveal patterns in her daily routine. For example, her runs on Tuesday and Thursday mornings and Sunday afternoons could allow someone to predict when she is likely to be out running.
Next, we will look at how information from Millie’s Strava profile could be connected to other sources, such as a fictional LinkedIn page. By combining these profiles, we can demonstrate how details that seem harmless on their own, such as someone’s school, graduation year, or running habits, can become more revealing when connected. We will also include a section about fitness-related information, showing what activity statistics like pace, distance, and frequency could reasonably suggest about someone, as well as the limitations of those conclusions.
Toward the end of the website, we want to bring these findings together to show how much information a viewer could potentially gather about Millie. We will distinguish between information that is directly visible, conclusions supported by repeated observations, and assumptions that cannot be confirmed.
Throughout the experience, we also plan to introduce relevant privacy settings alongside the risks they address. For example, after demonstrating how a starting location could suggest someone’s home, we can explain how Strava’s Map Visibility settings help limit this exposure. We want the final section to summarize these protections and encourage readers to review their own privacy settings.
By the end of the website, our goal is for readers to understand not only how much information can be inferred from something as ordinary as a Strava run, but also what they can do to make more informed decisions about the information they share. Our intended audience is primarily our classmates, but we also want the website to be useful to a general audience, especially young adults who use Strava and other social platforms with location-sharing features.

4.2 Implementation Progress
	We have completed our initial research, developed our fictional runner Millie, and outlined the website's narrative structure. We have identified the privacy risks we want to demonstrate and how each section will build on the previous one.
We have also selected our implementation tools: Scrollama for scrolling interactions, MapLibre GL JS for the route visualization, Turf.js for tracking movement along the route, D3 for additional visualizations, and Vercel for deployment. While we have not yet begun building the website, our next priority is implementing the map and connecting it to the scrolling narrative.

4.3 Deviations, Challenges, and Revised Plans

By October 28, we had completed our initial research and determined our website structure. We made two changes to our original scope: removing camera matching due to ethical concerns and adding social connections and aggregate exposure. Our main obstacles have been limited Strava API access, designing compelling visualizations, and managing implementation time.
During our consultation with Sachi, our Computing Fellow, we confirmed that Strava's API would not support our intended use. We discussed alternatives, including creating simulated GPX routes, using OpenStreetMap data, and designing mock profiles. We are also studying interactive pieces from The New York Times and The Pudding for visualization ideas and prioritizing essential features over more ambitious interactions.
